#26 ISIS Territorial Defeat Campaign National Security / Counterterrorism76/100

Overview

What Happened

When Trump took office, ISIS had already lost substantial ground but still held territory, fighters, revenue sources, and propaganda value in Iraq and Syria. The administration continued Operation Inherent Resolve, delegated more tactical authority to commanders, relied heavily on Iraqi forces and the Syrian Democratic Forces, and supported those partners with U.S. airpower, intelligence, special operations, logistics, and coalition coordination. By March 2019, the last ISIS-held village of Baghuz had fallen, ending the territorial caliphate ISIS had proclaimed in 2014.

Why It Happened

The objective was to destroy ISIS as a governing entity, not merely contain it. Trump criticized the pace of prior operations and favored a more aggressive military tempo, while military planners continued a coalition strategy already underway. The administration also wanted to prevent ISIS from using territory to train recruits, collect taxes, produce propaganda, and direct or inspire attacks abroad.

Immediate Effects

The campaign liberated territory and populations that had lived under ISIS rule, removed the group from major urban strongholds, and reduced its ability to operate as a proto-state. It also created immediate stabilization problems: displaced civilians, destroyed cities, detention of ISIS fighters, governance vacuums, Kurdish-Turkish tensions, and reconstruction needs.

Long-Term Consequences

ISIS survived as an insurgent and terrorist network, and affiliates remained active elsewhere. Still, the loss of territorial rule was a major counterterrorism milestone. The campaign ranks below Top 25 domestic and institutional policies because fewer Americans were directly affected, but it remains one of the most significant national-security outcomes of the Trump era.

Impact Score 76/100
Population Affected

Millions of civilians in Iraq and Syria, coalition forces, Iraqi and Kurdish partner forces, refugees, detainees, regional governments, and global counterterrorism agencies.

Economic Magnitude

Defense operations, reconstruction, humanitarian assistance, regional stabilization costs, and the loss of ISIS revenue from territory, taxation, oil, and extortion.

Supportive Analysis

Supporters argue that the administration accelerated battlefield momentum by loosening operational constraints and giving commanders more flexibility. They credit the campaign with destroying the unique feature that made ISIS especially dangerous: territorial control.

Supporters also emphasize that the United States achieved the objective without a massive new ground war. Local partners bore much of the fighting burden while U.S. forces supplied airpower, intelligence, and coalition coordination.

A third supportive argument is deterrent and symbolic. Eliminating the caliphate reduced ISIS recruitment appeal, disrupted its revenue model, and demonstrated that an extremist organization attempting statehood could be dismantled militarily.

Critical Analysis

Critics argue that the victory was the culmination of a campaign begun under Obama, so attributing it primarily to Trump oversimplifies years of coalition work. The strategy, partner relationships, and many operational gains predated 2017.

Critics also argue that territorial defeat did not solve the political conditions that enabled ISIS: sectarian governance, weak institutions, economic desperation, and regional proxy conflicts.

A third criticism concerns the aftermath. Stabilization, detainee management, Kurdish security, reconstruction, and U.S. troop posture remained unresolved, leaving space for ISIS remnants to persist.

#27 Soleimani Strike and Iran Escalation National Security / Iran Policy75/100

Overview

What Happened

On January 3, 2020, a U.S. drone strike near Baghdad International Airport killed Qassem Soleimani, commander of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps-Quds Force, along with Iraqi militia leader Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis. Soleimani was central to Iran's regional proxy network, including relationships with militias in Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Yemen. The strike followed attacks on U.S. personnel and facilities in Iraq and a confrontation at the U.S. Embassy compound in Baghdad.

Why It Happened

The administration said the strike was intended to stop imminent threats to Americans and restore deterrence against Iran and its proxies. Trump officials argued that Soleimani had orchestrated attacks for years and that prior restraint had encouraged escalation. Critics questioned the imminence claim and argued that killing a senior state military official on Iraqi soil risked war without congressional authorization.

Immediate Effects

Iran retaliated with ballistic missile strikes on U.S. forces at Al Asad Air Base and another facility in Iraq, causing traumatic brain injuries though no immediate U.S. deaths. The strike also led Iraq's parliament to call for removal of U.S. troops, heightened regional alerts, and contributed to the accidental Iranian shootdown of Ukraine International Airlines Flight 752 amid crisis conditions.

Long-Term Consequences

The strike changed deterrence debates but did not settle U.S.-Iran conflict. Iran continued regional pressure, proxy activity, and nuclear escalation after the U.S. withdrawal from the JCPOA. The later 2025-2026 Iran escalation displaced this event as the more consequential Iran entry, but Soleimani remains a defining first-term national-security action.

Impact Score 75/100
Population Affected

U.S. forces in Iraq and the region, Iranian and Iraqi officials, regional militias, Iraqi civilians, air travelers affected by crisis fallout, Congress, and allies monitoring escalation risk.

Economic Magnitude

Military costs, regional security costs, oil-market risk, diplomatic disruption, force-protection costs, and indirect economic consequences from escalation concerns.

Supportive Analysis

Supporters argue that Soleimani was responsible for the deaths of Americans and regional destabilization, and that killing him restored deterrence. They contend that Iran's Quds Force operated through deniable proxies and that a direct strike disrupted that model.

Supporters also argue that the limited Iranian response showed that decisive action could impose costs without triggering full-scale war. From this perspective, restraint after repeated militia attacks had become more dangerous than force.

A third supportive argument is moral and strategic: removing a uniquely capable commander weakened Iran's regional network and signaled that U.S. personnel could not be attacked with impunity.

Critics argue that the strike risked a major war and relied on contested claims about imminent threats. They warned that killing a senior Iranian official crossed a threshold that could normalize assassination as policy.

Critics also emphasize Iraqi sovereignty. The strike occurred in Baghdad and killed an Iraqi militia leader, complicating the U.S. military presence and strengthening calls for withdrawal.

A third criticism is that deterrence gains were temporary. Iran continued nuclear and regional escalation, and the United States remained caught between pressure, retaliation, and diplomacy.

#28 Travel Ban / Entry Restrictions Immigration / National Security74/100

Overview

What Happened

In January 2017, Trump issued an executive order temporarily restricting entry from several Muslim-majority countries and suspending refugee admissions. The rollout caused airport detentions, protests, confusion among agencies, and immediate litigation. Courts blocked early versions, and the administration revised the policy. In 2018, the Supreme Court upheld a later version in Trump v. Hawaii, allowing restrictions on nationals from several countries based on a national-security review process.

Why It Happened

The administration argued that the restrictions were necessary to improve vetting for countries with weak information-sharing, terrorism concerns, or unstable governments. Trump had campaigned on restricting entry from regions associated with terrorism, and the policy reflected his broader view that immigration and national security should be linked more aggressively.

Immediate Effects

The policy affected visa applicants, refugees, families, students, workers, universities, hospitals, employers, and U.S. citizens with relatives abroad. The first rollout caused immediate disruption at airports and consulates. Later versions were more legally structured but still separated families and complicated travel, study, and employment for affected nationals.

Long-Term Consequences

The travel ban's long-term importance lies in law and symbolism. The Supreme Court decision gave presidents substantial deference in immigration and national-security entry restrictions, even amid claims of discriminatory intent. Biden later revoked the ban, but the precedent and political model remain relevant for future presidents.

Impact Score 74/100
Population Affected

Visa applicants, refugees, families, students, workers, universities, hospitals, employers, U.S. citizens with relatives abroad, consular officers, and nationals of designated countries.

Economic Magnitude

Economic effects included lost travel, tuition, labor mobility, medical staffing, business disruption, litigation, and administrative costs, though the policy was smaller in fiscal scale than tax or trade measures.

Supportive Analysis

Supporters argue that the president has broad authority to restrict entry when national-security vetting is uncertain. They contend that countries unable or unwilling to share reliable identity and security information present risks that immigration officials should not ignore.

Supporters also say the final version was not a religion-based ban but a country-specific security policy that included non-Muslim-majority countries and allowed waivers. The Supreme Court's decision is cited as validation that the policy fit within statutory presidential authority.

A third supportive argument is precautionary: entry screening must prevent rare but catastrophic failures, and temporary restrictions can create leverage for better information-sharing.

Critical Analysis

Critics argue that the policy was rooted in anti-Muslim campaign rhetoric and stigmatized entire nationalities and religious communities. They contend that the legal revisions sanitized, but did not erase, discriminatory intent.

Critics also emphasize human consequences: families separated, students and workers stranded, refugees delayed, medical professionals blocked, and U.S. citizens unable to reunite with relatives.

A third criticism is that nationality-based restrictions are crude security tools. Individualized vetting, intelligence sharing, and targeted watchlists may be more effective than broad country bans.

#29 Refugee Admissions Reductions Immigration / Humanitarian Policy73/100

Overview

What Happened

The Trump administration reduced the annual refugee admissions ceiling to historically low levels, including a ceiling of 18,000 for fiscal year 2020 and 15,000 for fiscal year 2021. It also paused admissions early in 2017, increased vetting, narrowed priorities, and created uncertainty for resettlement agencies whose funding and staffing depend on arrivals. The policy reversed decades in which the United States had often been the world's largest refugee resettlement country.

Why It Happened

The administration argued that refugee admissions required stricter security screening, that local communities deserved more say, and that humanitarian resources should focus more on helping displaced people closer to home. The policy also fit Trump's broader effort to reduce immigration flows and link admissions to national security.

Immediate Effects

The immediate effects were dramatic for refugees already in the pipeline and for domestic resettlement infrastructure. Families waited longer, some cases expired or were delayed, local agencies closed offices or reduced staff, and communities that had built resettlement capacity lost arrivals. Religious groups, veterans' groups, and humanitarian organizations objected strongly.

Long-Term Consequences

The long-term consequence was institutional damage to the resettlement system. Refugee admissions can be raised again, but rebuilding overseas processing, local affiliates, interpreters, caseworkers, and trust takes time. The reduction also changed global perceptions of U.S. humanitarian leadership.

Impact Score 73/100
Population Affected

Refugees, family members, U.S. resettlement agencies, local communities, religious charities, employers, schools, interpreters, foreign-policy partners, and displaced populations seeking durable resettlement.

Economic Magnitude

The fiscal scale is modest compared with major federal programs, but the policy affected resettlement funding, local services, labor contributions, humanitarian commitments, and the infrastructure of refugee agencies.

Supportive Analysis

Supporters argue that lower admissions allowed more careful vetting and reduced pressure on communities and agencies. They contend that refugee resettlement should be aligned with national security and local capacity.

Supporters also argue that helping refugees closer to conflict regions can assist more people per dollar than U.S. resettlement. From this perspective, resettlement should be reserved for the most urgent cases.

A third supportive argument is democratic accountability: localities and states should have a voice in whether and how resettlement occurs because schools, housing, health care, and social services are local concerns.

Critics argue that the reductions abandoned a core U.S. humanitarian commitment and harmed people who had already undergone extensive screening. Refugees are among the most vetted entrants to the United States.

Critics also emphasize damage to resettlement infrastructure. When arrivals collapse, agencies lose staff and offices, making future rebuilding slow even if policy changes.

A third criticism is strategic: refugee resettlement can support allies, protect interpreters and vulnerable partners, and demonstrate soft power. Reductions weakened that signal.

#30 Remain in Mexico (Migrant Protection Protocols) Immigration / Asylum / Border Policy72/100

Overview

What Happened

The Migrant Protection Protocols, widely known as Remain in Mexico, began in 2019. Under the policy, certain non-Mexican asylum seekers arriving at the southern border were returned to Mexico to wait for U.S. immigration court hearings instead of being released into the United States. The policy required coordination with Mexico, new hearing logistics, border transportation, tent courts, and procedures for fear screenings. It became one of the most visible Trump asylum policies.

Why It Happened

The administration argued that many asylum seekers were using weak processing rules to enter the United States and remain for years while cases moved slowly through backlogged courts. By requiring applicants to wait in Mexico, officials sought to reduce incentives for irregular migration, discourage weak claims, and relieve pressure on detention facilities and Border Patrol.

Immediate Effects

The immediate effects were sharp. Tens of thousands of asylum seekers were returned to Mexican border cities, often living in camps, shelters, or precarious housing while trying to attend U.S. hearings. Border crossings and asylum patterns shifted, but legal access became harder because many applicants had difficulty finding U.S. lawyers, gathering evidence, or safely appearing at hearings.

Long-Term Consequences

The long-term consequences include a lasting fight over whether asylum seekers should be allowed into the United States while claims are adjudicated. Biden ended the policy, courts and states contested termination, and later Trump-era planning revived similar concepts. MPP became a template for externalizing asylum obligations and negotiating border enforcement with neighboring countries.

Impact Score 72/100
Population Affected

Asylum seekers, migrants, families, border communities, Mexican cities, U.S. immigration judges, lawyers, shelters, DHS personnel, and U.S.-Mexico diplomatic actors.

Economic Magnitude

The economic scale includes border operations, courts, shelters, enforcement, legal services, municipal costs, and labor-market effects, though the main impact was humanitarian and institutional rather than fiscal.

Supportive Analysis

Supporters argue that MPP reduced incentives for migrants to make weak asylum claims as a way to enter and remain in the United States. They contend that the policy restored consequences to a system overwhelmed by backlogs.

Supporters also argue that it reduced pressure on detention space and Border Patrol facilities. If applicants waited outside the United States, federal agencies faced fewer releases and less overcrowding.

A third supportive argument is diplomatic: MPP forced regional cooperation and made Mexico a more active partner in managing migration flows.

Critical Analysis

Critics argue that MPP exposed asylum seekers to kidnapping, assault, extortion, homelessness, and dangerous border-city conditions. Human-rights groups documented serious harms among people returned to Mexico.

Critics also argue that the policy undermined due process. Many asylum seekers lacked lawyers, missed hearings because of danger or confusion, and struggled to collect evidence from outside the United States.

A third criticism is legal and moral: asylum law is intended to protect people fleeing persecution, and forcing them to wait in unsafe conditions can make protection practically unreachable.

#31 Public Charge Rule Immigration / Welfare71/100

Overview

What Happened

The Trump administration expanded public-charge standards through a 2019 DHS rule that treated use of certain noncash benefits, income, health, age, education, and English proficiency as relevant to admissibility and adjustment of status. The rule did not apply to every immigrant category, but its practical effects extended beyond formal coverage because many families feared that using Medicaid, SNAP, or housing support could harm immigration prospects.

Why It Happened

The administration argued that immigrants seeking permanent residence should be self-sufficient and not likely to depend on public benefits. Supporters viewed the rule as a restoration of an older principle in immigration law and a protection for taxpayers. Critics argued that the policy created a chilling effect, causing eligible families, including U.S.-citizen children in immigrant households, to avoid health and nutrition programs.

Immediate Effects

The immediate effects included litigation, confusion among applicants, changes in legal counseling, and reported drops or hesitancy in benefit participation among immigrant families. The Biden administration later reversed the Trump approach, but the episode remains important because it showed how administrative immigration rules can shape behavior even before final adjudication.

Long-Term Consequences

The long-term consequence is a recurring fight over whether immigration policy should evaluate economic self-sufficiency narrowly or broadly. The rule ranks below Remain in Mexico because it was less visible at the border, but above Title 42 because its chilling effect reached deep into settled immigrant communities.

Impact Score 71/100
Population Affected

Immigrant families, green-card applicants, U.S.-citizen children in mixed-status families, hospitals, public-benefit agencies, schools, legal-service providers, and state governments.

Economic Magnitude

Economic effects included benefit participation, uncompensated care risk, administrative compliance, legal services, and household decisions around health care, nutrition, and housing assistance.

Supportive Analysis

Supporters argue that Public Charge Rule addressed a real policy failure rather than a merely symbolic concern. They emphasize enforceability, administrative clarity, and the need for the federal government to set workable rules in immigration / welfare.

A second supportive argument focuses on incentives. Supporters contend that clear consequences or clearer eligibility rules can reduce abuse, improve resource allocation, and make the system more predictable for officials and the public.

Supporters also argue that critics often understate operational pressures faced by agencies. From this view, the policy was an attempt to restore order to systems that had become overloaded, legally uncertain, or politically unsustainable.

#32 Title 42 Border Expulsions Immigration / Public Health71/100

Overview

What Happened

In March 2020, CDC issued an order under Title 42 allowing rapid expulsion of many migrants at land borders on public-health grounds. The policy operated outside ordinary immigration removal procedures and sharply limited access to asylum screening. It continued into the Biden administration before ending in 2023, making it one of the most consequential pandemic-era border policies.

Why It Happened

The administration argued that congregate border facilities and cross-border migration created unacceptable public-health risks during the early pandemic. Supporters said rapid expulsions protected Border Patrol agents, migrants, and communities while hospitals and agencies were under severe strain. Critics argued that the policy used health authority as a substitute for immigration authority and violated refugee-protection norms.

Immediate Effects

The immediate effects included hundreds of thousands of rapid expulsions, repeated crossing attempts by some migrants, reduced access to asylum hearings, and increased pressure on Mexican border communities. Families, single adults, and unaccompanied minors were affected differently as court rulings and agency choices shifted implementation.

Long-Term Consequences

The long-term consequence is a precedent for using emergency health law to control migration. It ranks below the public charge rule because Title 42 was formally temporary, but above DACA rescission because its operational effect at the border was massive.

Overview

What Happened

In 2017, the Trump administration announced the rescission of Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, the Obama-era program protecting certain undocumented immigrants brought to the United States as children from removal and allowing work authorization. The rescission triggered nationwide litigation and a 2020 Supreme Court decision holding that the administration's action was arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act.

Why It Happened

The administration argued that DACA was legally defective because it had been created by executive action rather than Congress. Supporters said ending it would restore constitutional boundaries and force Congress to legislate. Critics argued that the rescission threatened people raised in the United States, many of whom were students, workers, parents, service members, or essential employees.

Immediate Effects

The immediate effects were legal uncertainty and partial program freezes. Existing recipients could renew under court orders, but new applicants faced shifting rules. Employers, universities, families, and recipients had to plan around the possibility that work authorization and protection from removal could disappear.

Long-Term Consequences

The long-term consequence is unresolved status. DACA survived the Trump rescission attempt but remains legally vulnerable without congressional legislation. It ranks below Title 42 because Title 42 affected active border operations on a larger scale, but above asylum procedure changes because DACA involved a clearly defined population deeply integrated into U.S. society.

#34 Asylum Procedure Restrictions Immigration70/100

Overview

What Happened

The Trump administration issued multiple asylum-related rules and policies beyond Remain in Mexico and Title 42. These included restrictions on asylum eligibility for people crossing between ports of entry, third-country transit bars, narrower interpretations of protected grounds, faster credible-fear screening, limits on work permits, and procedural changes affecting immigration courts. Many were blocked, narrowed, or reversed, but together they represented a systematic effort to tighten asylum.

Why It Happened

The administration argued that the asylum system was being overwhelmed by weak or fraudulent claims and that long backlogs encouraged irregular migration. Supporters said stricter rules would preserve asylum for people with stronger claims while discouraging use of asylum as a general migration pathway. Critics argued that the changes undermined statutory and treaty-based protections for people fleeing persecution.

Immediate Effects

The immediate effects included confusion at the border, litigation, changing screening standards, reduced access to counsel, and uncertainty for applicants. Different policies applied at different times depending on courts and agency guidance, making the asylum system more difficult for applicants and lawyers to navigate.

Long-Term Consequences

The long-term consequence is a more contested asylum baseline. Even after reversals, later administrations inherited a debate over whether asylum should be narrowed administratively or expanded with more capacity. This ranks below DACA because the affected population was more fluid and many rules were blocked, but above family separation because the procedural system-wide effect was broader.
